Proposition: Force allegations are substantially less likely to be substantiated than other types of police misconduct allegations.

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

Scores range from -2 (fully deceptive) to +2 (fully earnest); negative scores indicate misleading framing and positive scores indicate earnest choices. Each section discusses both panels of its visualization.

  • Use all outcomes in the substantiation-rate denominator (score: +2). The left panel divides substantiated allegations by all allegations in each category, including exonerated outcomes, so Force's 12.8% can be compared directly with 16.9%, 21.9%, and 30.4%. This answers the proposition's question about likelihood more directly than raw counts; excluding exonerated allegations is an alternative, but would change the population being compared. The full denominator preserves that context, although these administrative findings describe recorded allegations, not the true prevalence of misconduct or the probability that an allegation is true.
  • Rank the bars and emphasize Force without distorting their lengths (score: +1.5). The left panel orders categories by their full substantiation rates, uses a zero baseline, and prints the percentages at the bar ends, making Force's last-place position easy to verify. Dark blue singles out Force against muted comparison bars, while the title immediately supplies the intended interpretation. Equal colors and alphabetical ordering would be less directive, but would make the relevant contrast slower to locate; the emphasis works well without changing the values, though the ranking alone does not establish what counts as a “substantial” difference.
  • Pool non-Force allegations into one benchmark (score: -0.5). The dashed 28.5% reference and the right panel's “All other types” line reduce three comparison categories to one, making the gap with Force look clear and consistent. Separate category lines would expose more variation but add visual clutter, so the pooled comparison supports a simpler two-group story. Its limitation is that a pooled rate is weighted by allegation counts: it can conceal category differences, and being below the combined line does not show that Force is below every individual category in every year.
  • Restrict the time series to 2000–2019 (score: -1). The right panel focuses on the period labeled as containing 95% of records, producing a readable twenty-year series in which Force remains below the pooled comparison throughout. Showing the full 1985–2020 span would also expose the sparse earlier years, where Force sometimes had a higher rate, so the selected window makes persistence more persuasive while limiting its scope. The date range and coverage annotation disclose the restriction, but the headline's “every year” should only be read as referring to the years shown.
  • Shade the gap and use a categorical headline (score: -1). The right panel combines two directly labeled lines with a filled band between them, so the reader sees a sustained separation instead of having to compare twenty pairs of points. A version with separate category lines and no fill would make the variation more visible; this design instead favors a memorable gap, while retaining a zero baseline. The headline “Force trails every other category, every year” goes beyond what the pooled line establishes, so the persuasive effect also creates a misleading implication that each individual category was checked and remained higher.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Exclude exonerated allegations from the denominator (score: -2). The left panel calculates the share substantiated among substantiated or unsubstantiated allegations, raising Force from 12.8% to 23.6% and placing it above Discourtesy (23.1%) and Offensive Language (16.9%). Retaining all outcomes would answer the original proposition more directly, but this conditional comparison is more effective for the opposing argument because it changes the ranking. The subtitle, axis, and source note disclose the restriction; nevertheless, using this result to challenge the likelihood across all allegations is deceptive because it changes the denominator rather than overturning the original comparison.
  • Place Force first and frame the result as “not the least” (score: -1). In the left panel, Force appears at the top in dark blue, immediately followed by the two categories it exceeds, while Abuse of Authority's much higher 42.8% appears last. A descending numerical order would put Abuse of Authority first and make Force's remaining deficit more prominent; the chosen order instead reinforces the headline “Force allegations are not the least substantiated.” This works as a ranking claim within the filtered outcomes, but Force's advantage over Discourtesy is only 0.5 percentage points, and “not the least” does not establish that Force is not substantially below the other categories collectively.
  • Switch from substantiation rates to absolute counts (score: -1.5). The right panel displays 976 substantiated Force allegations, exceeding Offensive Language's 127 and approaching Discourtesy's 1,026, which makes Force look like a meaningful part of substantiated misconduct. Rates or paired counts and denominators would be better suited to comparing likelihood; counts were used here because the volume provides a stronger opposing narrative. Although the count axis and source note identify the metric, different category totals mean that these correct counts cannot refute the proposition about probability.
  • Lead with “Hundreds” and select a favorable comparison (score: -1). The right panel's headline turns 976 into the memorable phrase “Hundreds of Force allegations were substantiated,” and its subtitle compares Force with Offensive Language, the smallest count. A headline reporting Force's rate, or a comparison with Abuse of Authority's 6,172, would make the opposing story less compelling; the selected wording instead emphasizes that substantiated Force allegations are numerous. The statement is numerically supported, but a large count can coexist with a low substantiation rate, so it invites an inference about likelihood that the panel does not establish.
  • Keep zero-based bars and exact labels, but acknowledge inconsistent color emphasis (score: +1). Both panels use horizontal bars starting at zero, readable category names, and exact value labels, allowing readers to check the selected comparisons without a truncated scale. This is more transparent than amplifying the small filtered-rate difference with a cropped axis. However, the right panel highlights Discourtesy in dark blue while Force is muted, reversing the left panel's emphasis; consistently highlighting Force would make the two panels easier to follow. The exact labels preserve interpretability, but this color inconsistency weakens the intended focus and could cause a reader who relies on color alone to misidentify Force's count.

Final Reflection

This project showed us that persuasive and deceptive visualizations do not necessarily depend on changing the underlying numbers. Instead, choices about filtering, denominators, aggregation, color, titles, and whether to display counts or percentages can substantially change the story a reader takes away from the same dataset. On the supporting side, the strongest technique was using the full substantiation rate, which directly matched the proposition and showed Force at 12.8%, below the other categories. Other choices, such as emphasizing Force with a darker color and grouping the remaining categories together, made the argument more persuasive while still preserving the core comparison.

The opposing side required more aggressive framing. Removing exonerated cases raised the Force rate to 23.6%, while switching from rates to absolute counts emphasized the 976 substantiated Force allegations. These values are technically correct, but they answer a somewhat different question from the proposition and can lead the reader toward a different interpretation.

The main lesson I learned is that a visualization can be technically accurate while still being misleading. The most important ethical issue is not only whether the numbers are correct, but whether the visual and statistical choices fairly represent the question being asked. I found the supporting side more trustworthy because it uses the full dataset and a metric that directly corresponds to the proposition, while the opposing side demonstrates how selective framing can create a persuasive alternative story.
